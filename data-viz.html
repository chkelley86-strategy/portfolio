Data viz library: visualizations that tell effective data stories at scale

HubSpot's reporting library could draw almost any chart, but it left users to find the meaning on their own, and every chart looked the same wherever it appeared. I led three initiatives to change that: consumer grade display options that add context to any report, contextually sensitive visualizations that adapt to where a report is viewed, and a redesign of the library itself.

The problem

A recurring theme in customer feedback was how hard it was to pull meaningful insights out of reports and dashboards.

"Currently the reporting feels very rigid and doesn't let me get an insight on what's really happening."HubSpot customer

Our 2023 Kano survey (105 responses) showed why this mattered to the business. NPS was strongly correlated with HubSpot's ability to help users make good decisions (r = .71). Users who gave HubSpot low decision-making scores had an NPS of -53, and users who gave high scores had an NPS of 38, a 91-point difference. Detractors also tended to be newer to HubSpot than promoters.

The goal was clear: help users understand their data better and get value from reporting faster, without asking them to build new reports or become data experts.

Part 1: Consumer grade display options

Display options are layers of context that sit on top of an existing report. With my product partners, I set the core requirements. Every option had to add context to a report, work without building a new report, apply across the whole platform, work for internal and external teams, and be controlled from the report itself.

We were just as clear about what we would not build: features that need deep technical expertise to set up, disrupt existing workflows, only serve one persona, or require big changes to other reporting surfaces. That is what "consumer grade" meant to us.

What we shipped

  • Trend lines: linear and logarithmic lines that answer time-based questions like "how have our sales grown this year?" at a glance.
  • Conditional formatting: diverging and sequential color scales in tables that use pre-attentive processing to point attention to what matters.
  • Anomaly detection: a model-driven expected range that flags data points that statistically stand out. It was first built for Data Quality, and we brought it to the whole platform.
  • AI Smart Trends: AI forecasts of future values for single and multi-dimension data, shown in the same frame as the observed data.

Design guides that scale

New display options kept arriving from different teams, so I wrote design guides that any team could follow. They are built on the reporting visualizations mission, "visualizations that tell effective data stories at scale," and four principles: develop hierarchy between primary and secondary data, highlight what is most important, tell a story or offer an opinion, and scale to solve specific problems for broad use cases.

Each guide pairs the background and design considerations with a single design decision, so teams do not have to reinvent the visual language:

  • Expected ranges are an area in the chart's own color at 60% transparency, so they stay secondary to observed data and work with custom palettes.
  • Expected values are a dashed line in the same color as the observed values.
  • Anomalies keep the shape and size of a normal point but add rings in the warning color, so they read as the primary data point.
  • Continuous color palettes reuse HubSpot's existing warning and reporting colors, at 50% opacity in tables to keep text and links readable.

Part 2: Contextually sensitive visualizations

As reporting spread across HubSpot, the same chart showed up in the report viewer, on dashboards, on homepages, in workspaces and on CRM cards. Our library was powerful, but it was one-size-fits-all. That made it harder to deliver easy, fast and unified experiences in each of those places.

I mapped each context by the job to be done. On one end, the report viewer is about analysis and exploration, with high data and chart detail. On the other, a CRM card is about getting a task done, with just enough data to explain what is happening.

Three ways to solve for context

The key idea was that one report configuration could serve every context. Instead of each team building its own charts, consuming teams could use the same report three ways:

  1. Multiple modes: the same report visualized as a lo-fi sparkline, gauge, benchmark or natural-language summary, or as a full chart.
  2. Configurability: components that teams and end users can configure to match the data density they need.
  3. Scaling to size: components that respond to the size of their container, scaling both their size and their data density.

Prioritizing with partner teams

I shared the concepts with the teams that build reporting into their own spaces and asked them to help rank priorities. Datawells came out on top. They were second only to tables in usage but had fallen the furthest behind: no scaling, limited configurability and no multiple modes. If we did not support them, teams would keep building their own off-platform "reporting" datawells.

Part 3: Redesigning the visualization library

When Trellis, HubSpot's design system, got a facelift, the data visualization library needed one too. I worked between the Trellis team and an outside agency to define the future of HubSpot's data visualization components, so the new charts would match the new system and leave room for display options and AI.

  • New chart anatomy: every chart is built from the same layers, from the background and frame up through the core marks, add-ons like reference and goal lines, interactive elements and an AI layer on top.
  • New color palettes and chart language: a refreshed visual style that fits the new Trellis look and keeps primary and secondary data easy to tell apart.
  • Room for AI and display options: forecasts, anomalies and insights have a defined place in every chart, so they feel like part of one cohesive reporting experience.
  • Design guidelines and resources so product teams can build with the new library on their own.

Impact

Display options were adopted by more than 100k users and improved two-week retention by 30%. Within weeks of launch, 13.1k portals had turned on linear trend lines and 11.8k had turned on logarithmic trend lines. Less engaged users who turned on trend lines had 10–13% higher two-week retention than those who did not. Anomaly detection and AI Smart Trends moved into beta in fall 2024, and the contextual visualization work set datawells as the next priority for the library.

What I learned

  • Say what you will not build. Defining "consumer grade" kept the roadmap focused on context for everyone, not power features for a few.
  • Design guides scale better than designs. One clear decision per element let other teams ship display options that look like they belong together.
  • Services over spaces. Building for one report configuration across many contexts made our library valuable to every team that shows data.
