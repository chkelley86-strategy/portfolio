Lead Scoring: AI building blocks that turn marketing signals into sales action

Marketers were guessing which signals mattered, and sales teams couldn't act on the scores they saw. I led design for a rebuilt, AI-first Lead Scoring app: modular components that recommend the right rules and explain their impact wherever users need them.

Where AI shows up

  • Discover AI rules: business intelligence surfaces the rules that actually convert in your portal, ranked by conversion rate and reach.
  • Confidence labels: every suggested rule shows how much data backs it, from high confidence to "needs more data."
  • Point recommendations: suggested point values that hit the intended spread of low, medium and high leads.
  • Rule insights: plain-language explanations of each rule's reach and impact, plus suggestions for similar signals.

The problem

AI changed the buyer journey from a funnel to an hourglass: fewer leads arrive, but they arrive informed and ready to buy. Prioritizing the right lead at the right time now matters more than volume, but HubSpot's lead scoring couldn't keep up.

Competitors were marketing instant setup and AI-built models. HubSpot's advantage was native data across the whole customer journey, but users couldn't get to it. Building a first score took more than two hours.

Pain pointWhat we heard
Marketers are guessing. No good starting point and no guidance on which signals matter."You need like a PhD in HubSpot to get things going."
Sales can't act. Little insight into what drives a score, and manual handoffs."Information is alright, but it's not fully actionable by the people that need to action on it."
Teams can't improve. Results are hard to interpret and iterate on."I just want to make sure that what I'm building works."

Research: mapping the whole job

Instead of studying only the builder screen, I mapped the full scoring journey with seven customers, from planning a score to refining it months later. They ranged from a 10-year HubSpot power user to a one-person marketing team.

What we learned

  • Users tested scores by hand. One marketer opened and clicked her own emails to "see do the scores work." Nothing in the product showed whether a model was right.
  • The handoff to sales was manual. MQL to SQL moves happened in calls, exported lists and Slack threads about "weird leads." Several sales teams could see the score but didn't use it.
  • Messy data distorted scores. Students, job seekers and other non-leads inflated engagement, so users kept tweaking rules.
  • People wanted set-and-forget, then a trend. They wanted to lock a model in and track month-over-month shifts.

The vision: three hills

With my PM and tech leads, we framed the 2026 vision around one persona, a growth-focused marketer, and three hills. Each hill names who benefits, what they can do, and why it matters. Each also has its own success metric, so the team could ship in phases and measure each one.

HillOutcomeTarget
Easy to startBuild a goal-aligned score with no expertise, using expert templates and rationale nudgesFirst score in under 30 minutes, down from 2+ hours
Confident to useSales sees why a lead is prioritized and acts on it in every toolHigher downstream activation of scores
Built to evolveVerify and improve the model against real outcomesUsers re-engage after score feedback

We also decided what not to do: no advanced multi-step ML training and no lead-object scoring.

Design: AI building blocks, not a linear wizard

The old Lead Scoring app worked in a silo: one long form you had to fill in top to bottom. Real users don't work that way. They start from scratch, come back months later, or dig into one rule and want to find better ones. So instead of a single flow, we designed modular AI components that can be called from different parts of the experience.

Research pointed to two moments where users stalled. The cold start: a blank builder and hundreds of properties, with no idea which few rules would move the needle. And the knowledge plateau: existing users stuck to obvious rules and never found high-converting signals outside their assumptions. The same two components solve both.

1. A starting point instead of an empty page

New users used to land on a blank page. The new empty state gives three ways in, and the most prominent is Discover AI rules.

2. Discover AI rules: every possible rule, ranked

Rather than make users hunt through hundreds of properties, AI lists every possible rule for the chosen goal and ranks it by impact: how many contacts it reaches, how many converted, and the conversion rate. Each rule carries a confidence label, so users know how much data backs the suggestion. They select the rules they want and add them to the score in one step.

3. Rule insights: see what each rule is doing

Once rules are in the score, users can open insights on any of them. Insights show how many leads a rule has reached, its impact on the overall score, and the average point distribution. That replaces the old habit of testing a score by hand.

The first design put insights in a panel on the right. From there, users could jump to the same Discover component, now filtered to rules similar to the one they were looking at.

4. The final design

After rounds of user feedback and working through technical limits, insights moved inline, directly under the rule they describe. Users see reach, average points and the distribution without losing their place, and "View similar workflows" opens Discover AI rules right where they are.

Impact

Lowering the friction to start a score led to 17% more scores being created. In total, 30k users have created 25k scores in the new app. Usage data also showed where to focus next: many users who start a score don't turn it on, which is exactly the gap the preview and confidence work is built to close.

What I learned

  • Map the whole job, not the screen. The builder wasn't where scoring failed. The handoff and the months after were.
  • Confidence is a design material. Users didn't need more rules. They needed to see that their rules worked.
  • Hills keep a big vision shippable. Three outcomes, each with its own metric, let us phase the work without losing the story.
